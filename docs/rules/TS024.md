# TS024 split-compound-assert

The severity is warning; the rule is on by default and stable.

## Reports

A compound assertion is split, so a failure names which half failed.

Reporting unit: Each assertion.

Anchor: The assertion call.

Carve-outs: A conjunction reports only at the condition's own depth. A disjunction at that
depth is one claim, and a negation that wraps the whole conjunction covers both halves at
once, so neither is split: `assert(!(a and b))` states `!a or !b`, which no pair of
assertions expresses, and `assert((a and b) or c)` is quiet for the same reason. A
disjunction nested inside a conjunct does not veto the split, so `assert(a and (b or c))`
reports, since `assert(a); assert(b or c);` expresses it. A negated conjunct is a different
shape and does report, because `assert(!a and b)` splits into `assert(!a); assert(b);` like
any other pair. A conjunction inside a nested call's argument list, a closure body, or a
macro body belongs to that scope rather than to the assertion.

The condition is the assertion's condition argument: the first argument, the argument
after the `t` of a Go testify helper such as `require.True(t, a && b)`, the argument of a
Node helper such as `assert.ok(a && b)`, and for a language whose assertions omit
parentheses the statement text up to its first comma, so Python's `assert (a) and (b)`
reports and `assert (a and b) or c` does not.

## Does not report

The rule reaches every language tigerstyle-lsp lints.

A finding is suppressed by a `tigerstyle-ignore:` directive naming TS024, by a
`tigerstyle-file-ignore:` directive, or by a `tigerstyle-disable:` region covering the line.

## Known false positives

The shapes that misfire here are `assert-helper-call` and `node-shape-anchor`.

## Citations

TigerStyle: "Split compound assertions: prefer `assert(a); assert(b);` over `assert(a and
b);`. The former is simpler to read, and provides more precise information if the
condition fails."
